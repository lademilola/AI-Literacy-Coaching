Why I do this

AI is moving faster than anyone's chance to understand it

The tools arrived first. The teaching never showed up. That gap is where I work, and it looks a little different depending on where you're standing.

In classrooms

Students have access to AI tools with no one to talk through the honest questions with: what counts as your own work, what the tool gets wrong, and when to check it against something real.

At work

AI shows up in people's jobs overnight, usually with a login and no training. Nobody explains what's safe to paste into it, where the data goes, or how bias slips into an answer that sounds confident.

At home

Older adults and families are meeting the risks of AI before the benefits. Scam calls can now copy a grandchild's voice. Meanwhile the tools that could read a prescription label aloud, translate a doctor's letter, or describe what is in a photo sit unused, because nobody sat down and showed them.

My work is making sure no one is left out of understanding AI and how the world will change.

About Me

I grew up in a brand new digital world. The next one should include all of us.

Hi, I'm Lea Gen Z AI Literacy Coach

I grew up as the internet and social media were taking over everyday life, and there was never a class for any of it. Nobody sat us down to explain what we were signing up for, what was safe to share, who was watching, or what we could believe. We worked it out by trial and error, in public, and some of those mistakes followed people for years.

At Howard University I studied Computer Information Systems, which taught me to see technology as a set of systems rather than magic. They are things built by people, with their choices baked in, that everybody else then has to live inside. And once you see it that way, you can't unsee who is left out.

AI is the next version of that moment, and it's arriving fast. I decided early that the generations coming up behind me shouldn't have to work it out alone the way we did, and neither should the ones who came before me. So I sit down with people and explain it in their own words, at their own pace.

You're not behind, but you do need to start now. This is just the beginning.

Almost everywhere I teach I hear the same two sentences: "I'm too old for this" and "I'm just not a tech person." I don't believe either one. What people usually mean is that nobody ever explained new technology to them without making them feel small, and that is a teaching and access problem, not a limit on what you can learn.

My sessions look nothing like training videos or lazy ebooks and courses. Instead of vocabulary lists, we open the real tools and use them on something from your own life, upholding ethics and truth along the way. For a fifth grader, that means learning to use AI without leaning on it to do the thinking. For a grandfather, it's an opportunity to analyze and action on the Medicare letter that nobody in the family can decode. For someone job hunting, it means understanding how companies use AI to screen applications, and then using it themselves to show up as a strong candidate. You can ask whatever you want, as many times as you want, because preparation takes time.

We also talk about the parts AI companies fail to share with you. These tools are not all-knowing magicians; they get things wrong and hand you false information with complete confidence. Your words go somewhere the moment you type them in, and you deserve to know where. Whole communities were missing from the data that trained them, and that leaves room for bias.

Understanding bias matters most for the people I sit with. Many of them are kids in underfunded schools, seniors living on a fixed income, or families in neighborhoods that get new technology last, never receiving the help to leverage it for their own benefit and prepare for the future.

By day I lead teams of engineers and technology professionals in retail engineering, turning complex ideas into things real people use at a scale where one small decision reaches millions of customers. Working that close to how technology gets built shows you how many people it quietly leaves behind. I believe access like mine comes with a responsibility to hand it back, and teaching others is how I plan to do that.

Who I teach

How I teach

Four things I don't compromise on

  1. 01

    Ethics first

    We talk about what's right alongside what's possible, in every session, starting in the first few minutes.

  2. 02

    Accessible to everyone

    You can walk in knowing nothing at all. Ask the same question three times and I'll answer it three times.

  3. 03

    Knowledge you keep

    You leave knowing how the thing actually works, so the next new tool is one you can figure out yourself.

  4. 04

    Practice over theory

    Hands on the tools, using your own examples, from the first few minutes.
